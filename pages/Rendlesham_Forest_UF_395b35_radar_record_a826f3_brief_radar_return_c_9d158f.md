## Introduction

A radar mark that appears for only three or four antenna sweeps is not, by itself, enough to demonstrate that a physical craft was present. In the context of the Rendlesham Forest incident, this question matters because one of the most frequently cited later claims is that a former RAF radar operator, Nigel Kerr, remembered seeing a brief [unexplained]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }}) return over RAF [Bentwaters]({{ 'bentwaters/' | relative_url }}). If accurate, that recollection would suggest that something unusual briefly appeared on a radar display. However, because no contemporary radar plot, tape, log entry or technical record survives to verify the event, the claim cannot be treated as confirmed radar evidence of a structured craft. The distinction is important: radar operators routinely evaluate fleeting returns against alternative explanations before classifying them as genuine targets, and that process cannot now be reconstructed from memory alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theufochronicles.com/2012/02/one-ufo-at-raf-bentwaters-appeared-on.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theufochronicles.com">[theufochronicles.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theufochronicles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">They Tracked an Otherworldly CraftFebruary 27, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 27, 2012</span></span></span>

## Can three radar sweeps prove a UFO?

The short answer is no. A genuine aircraft can certainly appear on only a few [radar]({{ 'radar/' | relative_url }}) sweeps if it is entering or leaving coverage, manoeuvring, or affected by terrain and radar geometry. Equally, non-aircraft phenomena can produce brief returns that disappear before they can be identified.

Military radar operators are trained to distinguish between:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* persistent, trackable targets;
* temporary echoes requiring further observation;
* clutter and anomalous reflections;
* equipment artefacts or interference.

</div>

A confirmed track normally depends on more than a momentary blip. Operators seek consistency across successive antenna rotations, agreement with known flight information where available, and [corroboration]({{ 'corroboration/' | relative_url }}) from other sensors or controllers. Without those checks, a brief response remains an unidentified radar contact rather than evidence that a physical craft occupied the reported position. General radar engineering likewise recognises clutter, atmospheric effects, multipath reflections and other transient echoes as routine challenges in interpreting radar displays.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_signal_characteristics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radar signal characteristics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radar signal characteristics</span></span></span>

### What Nigel Kerr reportedly remembered

The principal source for the "three or four sweeps" story is not a contemporaneous military document but a much later interview reported by UFO researcher Georgina Bruni and subsequently repeated by other authors.

According to that account, former RAF Watton radar operator Nigel Kerr recalled receiving a telephone call from Bentwaters asking whether anything unusual appeared on radar. He reportedly looked at his display, observed a return directly over the base for three or four antenna sweeps, watched it disappear, and never filed an official report because the contact vanished almost immediately. Kerr later said he only connected the incident with Rendlesham years afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theufochronicles.com/2012/02/one-ufo-at-raf-bentwaters-appeared-on.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theufochronicles.com">[theufochronicles.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theufochronicles.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">They Tracked an Otherworldly CraftFebruary 27, 2012...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 27, 2012</span></span></span>

If accepted at face value, Kerr's recollection suggests that he believed he briefly saw an unexplained radar response. It does **not** establish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* what produced the return;
* whether it represented a solid airborne object;
* whether another radar also detected it;
* whether technical checks excluded ordinary explanations;
* whether the return matched the witnesses' visual observations in time or location.

</div>

The absence of those supporting details limits the evidential weight of the recollection even if Kerr accurately remembered seeing an unusual display.

## Common causes of fleeting radar marks

Brief radar returns are not inherently extraordinary. Radar systems can generate temporary responses for several reasons that require investigation before an operator concludes a real aircraft is present.

Some common possibilities include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Ground clutter.** Buildings, trees or terrain can generate echoes under particular conditions.
* **Weather and atmospheric propagation.** Temperature inversions and unusual propagation can produce unexpected reflections or extend radar coverage in misleading ways.
* **Birds or biological targets.** Flocks of birds occasionally generate transient radar responses.
* **Multipath reflections.** Radio waves reflecting from the ground or other structures may create false target positions.
* **Electronic noise or processing artefacts.** Short-lived receiver effects can appear as isolated returns.

</div>

The key point is not that any one of these explanations definitely accounts for the alleged Bentwaters return, but that professional radar interpretation requires excluding such possibilities before assigning a contact to a real airborne object. A contact lasting only a few sweeps offers much less opportunity for those checks than one that develops into a stable radar track.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_signal_characteristics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Radar signal characteristics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radar signal characteristics</span></span></span>

## Why no preserved plot limits the claim

The strongest limitation on the alleged radar contact is not that it was brief, but that no contemporaneous record has been found.

Official Ministry of Defence responses have consistently stated that available records contain no radar reports supporting Colonel [Charles Halt]({{ 'charles-halt/' | relative_url }})'s account and that no radar tapes relating to Rendlesham have been identified. Parliamentary answers have likewise stated that officials found no radar reports or recordings confirming an unidentified object during the incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2015-07-16/HL1550" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: questions-statements.parliament.uk">[UK Parliament]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">questions-statements.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament Written questions and answers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentWritten questions and answers - Written questions, answers and statements - UK Parliament...</span></span></span>

Released defence documents also indicate that contemporary examinations of logs found no entries describing unusual radar returns. ## What the evidence ultimately supports

The later testimony attributed to Nigel Kerr remains an interesting piece of retrospective evidence because it suggests that at least one experienced radar operator believed he briefly observed something unexpected. However, retrospective recollection and contemporaneous technical documentation carry different evidential weight.

Within the surviving historical record of the Rendlesham incident, no preserved radar track, log entry, recording or independent operational record confirms that a structured craft crossed the airspace over Bentwaters or [Woodbridge]({{ 'woodbridge/' | relative_url }}). Consequently, the alleged three- or four-sweep return cannot, by itself, prove that a physical craft was present. At most, it represents an unverified recollection of a brief unexplained radar response that cannot now be independently tested against the records normally required to establish a confirmed radar target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2015-07-16/HL1550" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: questions-statements.parliament.uk">[parliament.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">questions-statements.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Parliament Written questions and answers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK ParliamentWritten questions and answers - Written questions, answers and statements - UK Parliament...</span></span></span>
