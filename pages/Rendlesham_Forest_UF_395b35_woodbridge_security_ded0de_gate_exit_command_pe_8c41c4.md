## Introduction

The patrolmen at RAF [Woodbridge]({{ 'woodbridge/' | relative_url }}) could not simply walk through the East Gate to investigate unusual lights because they were performing a regulated security duty, not acting as private individuals. During the Cold War, security police were responsible for guarding an operational United States Air Force installation. Leaving a designated post, especially during the night, required authorisation through the established chain of command so that the base remained protected, responsibility was documented, and any investigation became an official security response rather than an unauthorised absence. This procedural detail is important because it shows that the initial search in Rendlesham Forest followed military command practices instead of being an impulsive pursuit of an unidentified object. The official record specifically notes that permission was requested and granted before the patrol crossed the perimeter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHalt_Memorandum.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Halt Memorandum.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Halt Memorandum.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJanuary 13, 1981</span></span></span>

## The Duty to Keep the Gate Manned

The East Gate was part of the security boundary surrounding RAF Woodbridge. Personnel assigned there were expected to maintain surveillance, control access and remain accountable for the area under their responsibility. Even when an unusual event occurred outside the fence, their first obligation was to the installation itself rather than to whatever lay beyond it.

This distinction mattered because crossing the gate changed the nature of the patrol's task. Inside the perimeter, security police operated under established defensive procedures. Outside the perimeter they entered civilian woodland where they no longer occupied a fixed security position and where movement had to be coordinated to avoid weakening the base's defences.

The wording of Lieutenant Colonel [Charles Halt]({{ 'charles-halt/' | relative_url }})'s memorandum reflects exactly this sequence. The patrolmen did not report leaving immediately after seeing the lights. Instead, believing that an aircraft might have crashed or been forced down, they first requested authority to investigate outside the gate. Only after the on-duty flight chief approved the request did three patrolmen proceed on foot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHalt_Memorandum.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Halt Memorandum.jpg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Halt Memorandum.jpg - Wikimedia CommonsJanuary 13, 1981</span></span></span>

1. Security police observed unusual lights beyond the perimeter.
2. They assessed the situation as potentially involving an aircraft accident or another security concern.
3. They contacted the on-duty flight chief rather than acting independently.
4. The flight chief authorised a patrol to leave the installation on foot.

Although the Halt memorandum records this sequence in only a few sentences, it reveals an important operational principle. Authority to alter a guard deployment rested with the supervising officer, not with the individual patrolmen. That ensured someone remained responsible for balancing the need to investigate against the need to continue protecting the installation.

The flight chief's approval also meant that any subsequent observations, radio communications and reports formed part of an accountable military action. Whether the lights ultimately proved significant or mundane, the patrol had been dispatched through recognised command procedures rather than through personal initiative. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHalt_Memorandum.jpg)

## Why Crossing the Perimeter Changed the Situation

The physical fence marked more than a property boundary. It also separated two different legal and operational environments.

Inside the base, security police exercised military authority over a controlled installation. Outside lay Rendlesham Forest and surrounding civilian land, where routine military guard responsibilities no longer applied in the same way. Leaving the installation therefore meant:

* temporarily abandoning a fixed defensive position;
* entering terrain outside normal military control;
* accepting additional safety and accountability considerations;
* creating an incident requiring command oversight.

The reported possibility of a crashed aircraft strengthened the case for authorisation rather than bypassing it. A genuine crash could involve injured personnel, hazardous materials or an ongoing security threat. Such circumstances required an organised response instead of an informal investigation. The permission granted by the flight chief therefore reflected standard command judgement under uncertain conditions rather than any assumption that the lights represented something extraordinary. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHalt_Memorandum.jpg)

## Why the Authorised Search Matters to the Record

The requirement for permission has become an important detail in later discussions of the Rendlesham Forest incident because it appears in the earliest official documentation, before decades of later interpretations and witness recollections.

The memorandum's sequence demonstrates that the military response began with an ordinary security assessment:

* unusual lights were observed beyond the perimeter;
* the observers considered a conventional explanation—a possible aircraft accident;
* permission was sought through the security chain;
* a supervised patrol was authorised.

This chronology undermines the idea that the initial patrol was dispatched because commanders had already concluded they were dealing with an unidentified flying object. Instead, the available documentary evidence indicates that the first decision was governed by established security procedures and command responsibility. Only after the authorised patrol entered the forest did witnesses report seeing the unusual object described in later accounts. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHalt_Memorandum.jpg)

The permission requirement also enhances the historical value of the official record. Because the patrol was formally authorised, the investigation became a documented military action rather than an off-duty anecdote. That administrative step helped produce one of the key primary documents associated with the Rendlesham Forest incident and provides modern researchers with a clearer reconstruction of how the event unfolded from the perspective of base security procedures.
