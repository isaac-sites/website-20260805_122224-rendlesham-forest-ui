## Introduction

Edward Cabansag matters because his early account of the first Rendlesham Forest search is notably less dramatic than the version that later made the incident famous. He described coloured lights, cautious movement through the forest and an initially puzzling glow, but his narrative ends with the patrol recognising ordinary lights at greater distances: an illuminated farmhouse and what he called a beacon light. He did not report examining a landed triangular craft, touching its surface or watching it depart.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/Rendlesham_Forest_UF_395b35_cabansag_witness_dif_e82dee-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Cabansag" width="1024" height="559" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

That difference does not prove that every light was mundane, nor does it establish that another witness invented his experience. It does show that the three servicemen involved did not emerge with one uniform account. Their statements differed from the beginning over what was seen, how close it was, where it was located and whether its apparent movement belonged to an object or resulted from observers moving through difficult terrain. Cabansag’s testimony is therefore central to judging how the Rendlesham story developed.

## Cabansag’s place in the search

Cabansag was an Airman First Class in the United States Air Force’s 81st Security Police Squadron. During the early hours of 26 December 1980, he accompanied Staff Sergeant Jim Penniston and Airman First Class John [Burroughs]({{ 'burroughs/' | relative_url }}) into Rendlesham Forest after unfamiliar lights were reported beyond RAF [Woodbridge]({{ 'woodbridge/' | relative_url }})’s East Gate. The men initially treated the situation as a possible aircraft accident or security emergency rather than as an extraterrestrial encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.skeptic.org.uk/2006/04/from-the-archive-the-truth-about-rendlesham/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skeptic.org.uk">[The Skeptic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skeptic.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Skeptic From the archive: The Truth about Rendlesham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The SkepticFrom the archive: The Truth about Rendlesham - The Skeptic...</span></span></span>

His role is sometimes obscured because later retellings concentrate on Penniston and Burroughs as the men who supposedly reached the closest point. Some reconstructions say Cabansag remained farther back while the other two continued ahead. Whatever his precise position at every stage, he was part of the investigating patrol and observed enough of the pursuit to provide an independent description of the lights, the route and the way their apparent nature changed as the men advanced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/apr/30/the-rendlesham-forest-mystery-its-the-perfect-storm-of-a-ufo-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

The surviving statement associated with Cabansag is especially valuable because it belongs to the small set of accounts prepared soon after the event, before books, documentaries and decades of public controversy reshaped the case. The original [first-night]({{ 'first-night/' | relative_url }}) statements were not widely available until researcher James Easton obtained and publicised them in the 1990s. Their appearance allowed the early testimony to be compared directly with later recollections rather than filtered through [Charles Halt]({{ 'charles-halt/' | relative_url }})’s memorandum or subsequent media accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.skeptic.org.uk/2006/04/from-the-archive-the-truth-about-rendlesham/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skeptic.org.uk">[The Skeptic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skeptic.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Skeptic From the archive: The Truth about Rendlesham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The SkepticFrom the archive: The Truth about Rendlesham - The Skeptic...</span></span></span>

There are limitations. Cabansag’s statement is signed but apparently undated, and he later reportedly said that he had signed it under “extreme duress”. That allegation raises a legitimate question about how freely the account was prepared, but the public evidence does not establish which words, if any, were dictated or altered. It is safest to treat the statement as important contemporary evidence without assuming either that it is flawless or that it was demonstrably falsified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/apr/30/the-rendlesham-forest-mystery-its-the-perfect-storm-of-a-ufo-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

## What Cabansag described

Cabansag’s account contains genuinely unusual observations. He reported red and blue flashing lights and a yellow light that appeared more constant. The patrol believed at first that the lights were within or immediately beyond the forest, which helps explain why they continued searching. His description therefore should not be reduced to “he saw only a [lighthouse]({{ 'lighthouse/' | relative_url }})”; the experience began with lights whose distance and identity were unclear to the men.

The decisive feature of his statement is what happened as they approached. Cabansag wrote that they concluded the lights must be beyond the woods because nothing corresponding to them appeared when they passed through the forest. A glow near the apparent beacon became identifiable at closer range as a lit farmhouse. The remaining light was then described as “only a beacon light off in the distance”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thecoldfile.com/articles/1980-rendlesham-forest/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thecoldfile.com">[thecoldfile.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thecoldfile.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1980 rendlesham forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cabansag’s statement is explicit: “We would see a glowing near the beacon light, but as we got closer we found it to be a lit-up farmhous...</span></span></span>

This sequence offers a mechanism for part of the incident: distance compression at night. In darkness, observers had few reliable visual cues for judging whether a bright light was tens of metres away among the trees or several kilometres away near the horizon. Branches intermittently blocking a fixed light could make it seem to blink, move sideways or change intensity. Walking towards it would alter the foreground alignment without necessarily bringing the source noticeably closer.

Cabansag’s description also indicates that more than one light source may have been involved. The illuminated farmhouse and the distant beacon were not necessarily the same thing as the coloured lights that first attracted attention. This distinction is important because conventional explanations do not require every observation to have a single cause. A patrol alerted by an initial transient light could subsequently focus on farmhouse lighting, the Orfordness lighthouse or other distant sources while believing that all belonged to one moving object.

Burroughs’ early statement broadly supports the pursuit element. He wrote that the men followed a rotating beacon for about two miles before recognising that it came from a lighthouse. Cabansag’s account similarly places the apparent target beyond the forest rather than at a landing site immediately in front of the patrol. Their descriptions are not identical, but they agree on the crucial change from an apparently nearby mystery to a distant fixed light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.ikwipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.ikwipedia.org">[ikwipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.ikwipedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>

## How his account differs from the others

The largest contrast is with Penniston’s later narrative. Penniston eventually described approaching a solid triangular vehicle, examining its black surface, observing symbols, touching the craft and watching it rise through the trees. He also introduced a claimed sequence of binary digits that he said entered his mind during physical contact. Cabansag reported none of those events.

The contrast is not merely between Cabansag’s early statement and Penniston’s decades-later memories. Penniston’s own initial report was also much more limited than his later version. It described a source of red and blue light and recorded that his closest approach was about 50 metres. It did not mention physical contact, a prolonged inspection, engraved symbols, a triangular hull or binary information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/apr/30/the-rendlesham-forest-mystery-its-the-perfect-storm-of-a-ufo-case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on theguardian.com.</span></span></span>

That creates three distinct levels of testimony:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Cabansag’s early account** emphasises coloured lights, a farmhouse glow and a distant beacon.
* **Burroughs’ early account** includes unusual lights, noises and movement in the woods, but also says the patrol eventually identified a lighthouse.
* **Penniston’s early account** describes an illuminated object at a distance, while his later accounts add a structured craft, close inspection and physical contact.

</div>

These are not minor variations in colour or timing. They concern the basic nature of the event. One witness’s account ends with distant lights; another retains a more ambiguous illuminated object; the most dramatic witness later describes a landed machine at arm’s length.

Charles Halt’s January 1981 memorandum further complicated the record by summarising the first-night object as triangular, metallic in appearance and apparently hovering or standing on legs. That wording is stronger than Cabansag’s statement and stronger in some respects than Penniston’s initial report. Because Halt did not personally witness the first search, this section of his memorandum was necessarily based on information supplied by others. It cannot by itself resolve which participant’s version was most accurate. The Ministry of Defence subsequently judged the affair to be of no defence significance, noting the lack of corroborating [radar]({{ 'radar/' | relative_url }}) evidence and the low-key manner in which it was initially handled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ufo-transcript-aug-09.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives</span></span></span>

Later supporters of the extraordinary interpretation have argued that the early statements were sanitised, incomplete or produced under pressure. The reported duress claim deserves acknowledgement, particularly because military statements may be shaped by command procedures. Yet it does not automatically validate every later addition. To demonstrate suppression, researchers would need independent evidence showing what Cabansag originally said, which passages were changed and who ordered the changes. Publicly available accounts do not provide that chain of proof.

## Why the differences matter

Cabansag’s statement changes the evidential balance because it prevents “the witnesses saw a landed triangular craft” from being treated as a collective observation. At most, that claim belongs principally to Penniston’s later testimony. Cabansag’s account does not corroborate it, while Burroughs’ early statement provides only partial and ambiguous support for a nearby illuminated phenomenon.

The disagreement also illustrates why witness sincerity and witness accuracy must be separated. All three men may have honestly reported what they believed they experienced. Night-time perception, uncertain distance, moving observers, intermittent views through trees and the expectation of a crashed aircraft could nevertheless produce different interpretations. A light seen through branches may look like a compact object to one observer, a collection of coloured sources to another and a distant beacon once the group reaches open ground.

Cabansag’s testimony is strongest as evidence of this changing perception. His account begins with unexplained coloured lights but records successive identifications as the patrol moves: the supposed nearby glow becomes a farmhouse, and the pursued light becomes a distant beacon. That progression supports explanations based on misjudged distance and the merging of separate visual stimuli more directly than it supports a single craft manoeuvring through the forest.

It does not settle every element of the first night. The precise source of the initial coloured lights remains disputed, and the documentary record is too incomplete to reconstruct every observer’s position minute by minute. Cabansag also did not necessarily share Penniston’s closest vantage point. His failure to describe a craft therefore cannot prove that Penniston saw nothing unusual outside Cabansag’s view.

Even with those qualifications, his statement sets an important limit on the case. The most spectacular Rendlesham narrative was not the patrol’s unanimous story, and it was not recorded in full in the earliest surviving testimony. From the outset, the witnesses differed over whether the lights were close or distant, structured or indistinct, moving or merely changing through the trees. Cabansag’s restrained account makes those differences impossible to treat as an insignificant detail: they are part of the central evidence that any explanation of Rendlesham Forest must address.
