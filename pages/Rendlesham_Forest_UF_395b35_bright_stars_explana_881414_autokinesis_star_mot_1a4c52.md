## Introduction

A stationary star can genuinely appear to dart, pause, reverse direction or trace small loops, even though it has not moved at all. This perceptual effect, known as the **autokinetic illusion** or **autokinesis**, occurs when a person stares at a single point of light against a dark, largely featureless background. Under these conditions, the visual system has few external reference points with which to judge position, allowing tiny involuntary eye movements to be misinterpreted as motion of the light itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15878673/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with f MRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with fMRI - PubMedAugust 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2005</span></span></span>

Within discussions of the Rendlesham Forest incident, autokinesis is relevant because some witnesses later watched isolated, bright, star-like lights low above the horizon for extended periods. While the illusion cannot explain every reported feature of the later aerial observations, it provides a well-established perceptual mechanism that can make a fixed celestial object appear to manoeuvre convincingly.

## What is the autokinetic illusion?

The autokinetic illusion is a recognised phenomenon in visual psychology in which a **stationary point of light appears to move when viewed in darkness**. It has been known for more than two centuries, with early observations attributed to Alexander von Humboldt while watching stars in the night sky. Modern laboratory experiments have repeatedly reproduced the effect using a single dim light in an otherwise dark room.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1053811905002168" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with f MRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with fMRI - ScienceDirectAugust 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2005</span></span></span>

The apparent motion is typically unpredictable. Observers may report that the light:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* drifts slowly in one direction;
* suddenly changes course;
* pauses before moving again;
* traces circles or zigzags;
* seems to surge forward or backwards.

</div>

Importantly, none of these movements occurs in the external world. The perceived motion is created by the interaction between normal eye movements and the brain's interpretation of visual information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholarship.richmond.edu/jepson-faculty-publications/162/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholarship.richmond.edu">[scholarship.richmond.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholarship.richmond.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Autokinetic Effect</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Autokinetic Effect</span></span></span>

## Why darkness and weak reference points matter

Under ordinary viewing conditions, the brain constantly compares the position of an object with surrounding landmarks such as buildings, trees, the horizon or neighbouring stars. These reference points make it easy to recognise that tiny eye movements have occurred while the scene itself remains stable.

In darkness, many of those anchors disappear. If an observer fixates on a single isolated light, there may be little or nothing against which to judge its true position. Small involuntary eye movements—known as microsaccades, drift and tremor—continue even during attempted steady fixation. Normally the brain compensates for these movements, but when visual references are extremely limited that compensation can become uncertain, allowing the light itself to appear to wander.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10965040/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Autokinesis reveals a threshold for perception of visual motion - PMC...</span></span></span>

Researchers do not regard the illusion as arising from a single simple cause. Modern studies suggest that both eye-movement signals and higher-level motion processing in the brain contribute. Functional MRI experiments have shown increased activity in the motion-sensitive visual area MT/V5 during periods when observers experience stronger autokinesis, even though the stimulus itself remains perfectly stationary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15878673/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with f MRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with fMRI - PubMedAugust 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2005</span></span></span>

## Why the movement feels convincing

One reason autokinesis is so compelling is that the perceived motion often appears spontaneous rather than imagined. Laboratory studies show that the illusion generally strengthens the longer a person continues staring at the same isolated target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15878673/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with f MRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with fMRI - PubMedAugust 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2005</span></span></span>

Several characteristics increase its persuasive quality:

* **Single bright target:** One prominent light is more likely to produce the effect than a richly structured star field.
* **Prolonged fixation:** The illusion often becomes stronger after several seconds or longer of continuous observation.
* **Minimal visual structure:** Featureless darkness reduces opportunities for the brain to recalibrate position.
* **Expectation and attention:** Psychological studies have found that observers' expectations can influence both the perceived amount and direction of movement, illustrating that [perception]({{ 'perception/' | relative_url }}) is not determined solely by the incoming visual signal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1053811905002168" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with f MRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Imaging the visual autokinetic illusion with fMRI - ScienceDirectAugust 1, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1, 2005</span></span></span>

The resulting motion can seem surprisingly real because the observer is not consciously aware of the tiny eye movements or the brain's corrective processes.

## How Rendlesham conditions could amplify apparent movement

The later aerial observations reported during the Rendlesham Forest incident occurred under several conditions known to favour autokinesis.

Witnesses described watching isolated, bright, "star-like" lights at night over a prolonged period. A dark forest environment provides relatively few stable visual references compared with an open landscape or an urban skyline. Looking towards a low object near the horizon can further reduce clear reference cues because atmospheric haze, darkness and distant terrain obscure fine detail.

These conditions do not prove that autokinesis occurred, but they match the circumstances under which the illusion is routinely demonstrated in experimental settings. If a bright star was already exhibiting strong atmospheric [scintillation]({{ 'scintillation/' | relative_url }})—rapid colour changes and flickering caused by turbulent air—autokinesis could add an impression of erratic lateral motion, abrupt stops or changes of direction. The combined effect would be more dramatic than either mechanism alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19880019247/downloads/19880019247.pdf?attachment=true" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Technical Reports ServerENGINEERINGApril 12, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 12, 2026</span></span></span>

## What autokinesis can and cannot explain

Autokinesis offers a plausible explanation for reports that a stationary celestial object appeared to make small, irregular movements. It is particularly relevant when witnesses describe:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* hovering lights that seem to drift unpredictably;
* sudden but limited directional changes;
* movement seen only during prolonged staring;
* motion that is difficult to record photographically.

</div>

However, the illusion also has clear limits.

It does **not** explain objects that demonstrably change position relative to multiple reference points, produce corresponding instrumented measurements, or are independently tracked by calibrated sensors. Likewise, autokinesis alone does not account for reports of structured craft, close encounters or physical interactions. It addresses only the perception of apparent movement in isolated lights.

For the Rendlesham case specifically, autokinesis is therefore best understood as one component of a broader naturalistic explanation. It complements, rather than replaces, other proposed mechanisms such as atmospheric scintillation of [bright stars]({{ 'bright-stars/' | relative_url }}) viewed low on the horizon.
