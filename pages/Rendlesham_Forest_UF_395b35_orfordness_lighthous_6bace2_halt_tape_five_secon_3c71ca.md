## Introduction

One of the strongest pieces of evidence supporting the Orfordness [Lighthouse]({{ 'lighthouse/' | relative_url }}) explanation is not simply that a lighthouse existed east of Rendlesham Forest, but that the principal flashing light recorded during Lieutenant Colonel [Charles Halt]({{ 'charles-halt/' | relative_url }})'s investigation appears to recur at almost exactly the same interval as the lighthouse's rotating beam. The Halt tape is unusual because it captures observations in real time rather than relying on recollections written days or years later. When listeners compare the timing of the group's repeated reactions to the distant light, a rhythm of roughly five seconds repeatedly emerges—the same flash period documented for the Orfordness Lighthouse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>

This timing evidence does not, by itself, prove that every light seen that night was the lighthouse. Instead, it provides a measurable dataset against which competing explanations can be tested. Unlike subjective descriptions of colour, shape or apparent movement, the interval between flashes is an objective feature that can be checked against contemporary recordings and the known characteristics of the lighthouse.

## What the tape records in real time

Charles Halt carried a portable cassette recorder while leading the second-night investigation on 28 December 1980. The recording captures the party moving through woodland and fields, commenting on lights ahead and occasionally stopping to observe them. Because the tape was made as events unfolded, it avoids many of the problems associated with later memory.

During the recording, members of the group repeatedly announce that the light has appeared again. The comments are separated by intervals that are close to five seconds. Researchers analysing the recording have noted that this cadence continues through multiple observation sequences rather than appearing only once by chance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>

The significance lies in the regularity. A naturally rotating lighthouse optic produces a highly stable flash period, whereas a genuinely manoeuvring airborne object would not ordinarily be expected to emit bright flashes at an unwavering five-second interval over an extended period unless it possessed an equally regular signalling system.

The tape also records Halt observing the light through a starlight scope, describing an intense flash with a dark centre. Image intensification devices can exaggerate bright point sources, causing them to bloom or display optical artefacts that differ from naked-eye appearance. Consequently, descriptions of the light's apparent structure are less useful for identification than the timing of its repeated appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wikidisc.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wikidisc.org">[WikiDisc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wikidisc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Wiki Disc Rendlesham Forest Incident &#124; Wiki Disc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest Incident &#124; WikiDiscDecember 26, 1980...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 26, 1980</span></span></span>

## Comparing reaction intervals with the lighthouse cycle

The Orfordness Lighthouse operated with a white flash every five seconds. Historical technical records describe this as the characteristic signature of its rotating optic, which was intended to identify the lighthouse uniquely to mariners. The flash interval therefore provides a distinctive reference point rather than a vague similarity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>

Researchers favouring the lighthouse explanation compare two independent datasets:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* the known five-second operating cycle of the lighthouse;
* the elapsed time between successive reactions on the Halt tape.

</div>

The correspondence is important because it relies on timing rather than interpretation. If listeners consistently measure approximately five seconds between visible reappearances of the light, the recording behaves exactly as expected for a distant lighthouse intermittently visible through trees or terrain.

Astronomer Ian Ridpath has argued that this is one of the strongest conventional explanations for the principal flashing light because it links the recorded observations directly to a documented navigational aid instead of relying solely on witness recollections. Later site visits from comparable viewing positions have also shown that the lighthouse could be seen through breaks in the tree line along the relevant bearing while it remained operational.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ianridpath.com/ufo/Halt_affidavit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ianridpath.com">[Ian Ridpath]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ianridpath.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ian Ridpath Col Halt&#x27;s affidavit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ian Ridpath Col Halt&#x27;s affidavit</span></span></span>

## Why the timing matters more than appearance

Descriptions of unidentified lights often vary between observers. Witnesses may disagree about colour, apparent size or whether a light seemed to move. Such characteristics are influenced by darkness, atmospheric conditions, expectation and the limitations of human vision.

Flash period is different because it can be measured.

The Halt tape therefore occupies a special place in discussions of the Orfordness explanation. Rather than asking whether a light "looked like" a lighthouse, investigators can ask whether it behaved like one in a quantifiable way.

This does not eliminate every uncertainty. The tape does not include an electronic timestamp for each flash, and reactions by observers introduce small delays. Nevertheless, repeated intervals clustering around the lighthouse's known cycle are generally regarded as stronger evidence than subjective descriptions of colour or apparent motion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>

## Limits and disputes in timing the flashes

Although the five-second correspondence is widely cited by sceptical investigators, it is not universally accepted as decisive.

Supporters of an anomalous interpretation argue that:

* the group may have been observing more than one light during the investigation;
* some reported movements or [apparent beams]({{ 'apparent-beams/' | relative_url }}) are not easily explained by a fixed lighthouse;
* observer reaction times make precise interval measurement imperfect.

Charles Halt himself later rejected the suggestion that the flashing light was simply the lighthouse, arguing that the observed object occupied a different position and displayed behaviour he believed was inconsistent with a stationary coastal beacon. Critics respond that these later recollections differ from the contemporary recording, which contains no explicit identification of a separate lighthouse and places the principal flashing light on the same general bearing as Orfordness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ianridpath.com/ufo/Halt_affidavit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ianridpath.com">[Ian Ridpath]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ianridpath.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ian Ridpath Col Halt&#x27;s affidavit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ian Ridpath Col Halt&#x27;s affidavit</span></span></span>

Another limitation is that the tape documents only part of the night's events. It cannot establish whether every reported light during the investigation shared the same origin. Consequently, even researchers who regard the five-second rhythm as compelling typically limit their conclusion to the recurring flashing light near the eastern horizon rather than claiming it resolves every aspect of the Rendlesham Forest incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thecoldfile.com/articles/1980-rendlesham-forest/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thecoldfile.com">[The Cold File]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thecoldfile.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1980 rendlesham forest</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Cold FileLights in the Pines: The 1980 Rendlesham Forest Incident &#124; The Cold File...</span></span></span>

## Why the five-second rhythm remains central

Among all the evidence discussed in connection with the Orfordness Lighthouse explanation, the recurring five-second cadence on Halt's recording remains one of the few features that can be examined objectively. The tape preserves not only what witnesses believed they were seeing but also when they reacted to it.

Because that timing closely matches the documented flash characteristic of the Orfordness Lighthouse, the recording has become a focal point in assessments of the conventional explanation. Whether one concludes that the lighthouse accounted for the entire observation or merely one component of a more complex sequence, the five-second rhythm provides a concrete benchmark against which competing interpretations must be measured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>
