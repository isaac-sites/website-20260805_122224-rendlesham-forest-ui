## Introduction

The radiation readings recorded during the Rendlesham Forest UFO incident did not show a clear or hazardous radioactive anomaly. Lieutenant Colonel [Charles Halt]({{ 'charles-halt/' | relative_url }})’s memorandum reported beta/gamma readings of up to **0.1 milliroentgens**, with lower readings on a nearby tree. However, the contemporaneous tape records mostly small, fluctuating indications around **0.03–0.04 milliroentgens per hour**, with a higher estimate of roughly **0.07** at one location. These values were close to the bottom of the survey meter’s range and were not collected using a controlled environmental survey.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHalt_Memorandum.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Halt Memorandum.jpgBeta/gamma readings of 0.1 milliroentgens were recorded with peak readings in the three de- pres...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. A meter needle moving slightly higher at one patch of ground does not by itself establish unusual contamination, still less prove that an unknown craft landed there. To support such a conclusion, investigators would have needed a calibrated instrument, repeat measurements, clearly defined control sites, documented [probe positioning]({{ 'probe-positioning/' | relative_url }}) and laboratory identification of any radioactive material. None of that survives in the Rendlesham record.

## How the survey meter was used

During Halt’s visit to the forest in the early hours of 28 December 1980, a member of his party used an **AN/PDR-27 radiac set** around three shallow depressions regarded as possible landing marks. Readings were also taken near the centre of the triangle formed by the marks and against a nearby tree. Halt narrated parts of the survey into his handheld recorder and later summarised the results in his “[Unexplained Lights]({{ 'unexplained/' | relative_url }})” memorandum.

The AN/PDR-27 was a portable military Geiger–Müller survey meter intended to detect beta radiation and measure gamma radiation. Versions of the instrument commonly offered ranges beginning at **0–0.5 milliroentgens per hour**, followed by progressively higher scales. At the two lowest ranges, radiation striking a Geiger–Müller tube produced electrical pulses; these were converted into audible indications and an averaged needle reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/collection/radiac/survey-meters/anpdr-27.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orau.org">[Oak Ridge Associated Universities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orau.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orau.org.</span></span></span>

This was a legitimate radiation-detection instrument, not a toy or improvised device. Yet its military pedigree does not mean that every small needle deflection was precise or significant. It was principally a field survey instrument for detecting contamination and potentially important radiation levels. Using the bottom fraction of its lowest scale to compare tiny variations in natural background radiation placed much greater demands on the equipment and operator than simply determining whether a substantial source was present.

The precise AN/PDR-27 variant used at Rendlesham has not been securely documented in the surviving contemporary material. Different variants had differences in construction and probe arrangement, although the family generally used separate detection arrangements for its lower and higher ranges. The probe’s beta window, its distance and angle from the ground, and whether it was held still long enough for the indication to settle could all affect a low-level result.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/collection/radiac/survey-meters/anpdr-27.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orau.org">[Oak Ridge Associated Universities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orau.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orau.org.</span></span></span>

## What the recorded changes meant

Halt’s memorandum states that beta/gamma readings of **0.1 milliroentgens** were recorded, with “peak readings” in the three depressions and near their centre. It gives readings of approximately **0.05–0.07** on the side of a tree facing the marks. Because the AN/PDR-27 displayed a rate, the intended unit was almost certainly milliroentgens **per hour**, even though the memorandum omitted the time element.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AHalt_Memorandum.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[Wikimedia Commons]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Halt Memorandum.jpgBeta/gamma readings of 0.1 milliroentgens were recorded with peak readings in the three de- pres...</span></span></span>

The tape presents a less tidy picture. The group discusses low readings, small “clicks” and brief needle movements while moving between the marks, the centre and nearby trees. Commonly cited reconstructions place much of the apparent background at about **0.03–0.04 mR/h**, with an estimate of approximately **0.07 mR/h** at the strongest recorded point. The tape also records an isolated increase elsewhere in the forest, away from the alleged landing area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ianridpath.com/ufo/halttape.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ianridpath.com">[ianridpath.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ianridpath.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ian RidpathRendlesham Forest UFO – the Halt tapeA transcript of Col Halt&#x27;s tape recording made during his sighting of the Rendlesham Fore...</span></span></span>

Several points limit what can be inferred from these numbers.

**The absolute values were small.** Even the memorandum’s highest figure, interpreted as 0.1 mR/h, was not evidence of a dangerous radiation field. It was a low environmental reading, not the kind of level that would independently indicate a powerful radioactive source or an immediate exposure hazard.

**The difference was only a fraction of the lowest scale.** On a 0–0.5 mR/h range, a reading of 0.05 occupies one-tenth of full scale and 0.1 occupies one-fifth. Small pointer movements in this region are much harder to interpret confidently than readings nearer the middle of the scale. AN/PDR-27 documentation for later variants specified accuracy over defined portions of each scale rather than guaranteeing precision at or near zero.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/416472923/An-Pdr-27s-Manual" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">An PDR 27s Manual &#124; PDF &#124; Troubleshooting &#124; Radiation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A unit of Radiac Set AN/PDR-27S... Accuracy ± 20%from 10%of full scale on each... occasionally deflect upscale slightly due to ambient...</span></span></span>

**Geiger readings naturally fluctuate.** Radioactive decay is statistical: pulses do not arrive at perfectly even intervals. The AN/PDR-27’s circuitry averaged the pulse rate before moving the needle, but at low count rates the display could still wander. A brief rise followed by a fall may therefore represent ordinary counting variation rather than a distinct source. The equipment manual itself explains that meter current is proportional to the average repetition rate of pulses generated in its Geiger–Müller tubes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://schaffrath.net/pdr-27/TM-11-6665-230-34.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: schaffrath.net">[schaffrath.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">schaffrath.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on schaffrath.net.</span></span></span>

**The memorandum is more definite than the tape.** The written claim that the three depressions contained the peak readings is not cleanly demonstrated by the spoken survey. At points, the tape suggests that the most noticeable movement occurred between or around the marks rather than consistently inside each one. This mismatch does not prove misconduct; Halt was condensing a confusing night-time investigation into a short memorandum. It does mean that the neat pattern described later should not be treated as an independently verified radiation map.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ianridpath.com/ufo/halttape.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ianridpath.com">[Ian Ridpath]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ianridpath.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ian RidpathRendlesham Forest UFO – the Halt tapeA transcript of Col Halt&#x27;s tape recording made during his sighting of the Rendlesham Fore...</span></span></span>

The most defensible interpretation is therefore that the party detected minor spatial variation in a low background field. The data do not establish whether the variation arose from natural radioactivity in soil and vegetation, instrument behaviour, changes in probe position, or some combination of these factors.

## Why measurement context matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

A scientifically useful environmental radiation survey requires more than a sequence of readings. It needs enough procedural detail for another investigator to reproduce the result and test whether the apparent pattern exceeds normal variation. The Rendlesham measurements lack several essentials.

</div>

There is no surviving calibration certificate identifying the particular meter’s condition on the night. AN/PDR-27 equipment required periodic calibration, and technical evidence from broader radiation-instrument comparisons stresses that careful calibration is necessary for dependable field measurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://schaffrath.net/pdr-27/TM-11-6665-230-34.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: schaffrath.net">[schaffrath.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">schaffrath.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on schaffrath.net.</span></span></span>

There was also no systematic baseline survey. The team did take readings outside the supposed landing marks, but the locations were selected while walking through the forest rather than through a pre-planned grid. No series of comparable control sites was measured at matched distances, heights and durations. Without that baseline, describing one value as “background” and another as a “peak” risks turning random local variation into an apparently meaningful pattern.

Probe geometry was not standardised either. A detector held against tree bark cannot be compared straightforwardly with one held above soil. Beta radiation has limited penetration, so measurements can change markedly with small differences in distance, shielding and whether the probe’s beta-sensitive window is open and facing the surface. The AN/PDR-27 family used its low-range detection system for beta as well as gamma radiation, making those details particularly important.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/collection/radiac/survey-meters/anpdr-27.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orau.org">[Oak Ridge Associated Universities]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orau.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orau.org.</span></span></span>

Environmental materials are not radiologically uniform. Soil can contain naturally occurring uranium-series products, thorium and potassium-40, while vegetation, moisture, stones and disturbed ground can alter a detector’s response over short distances. UK radiation exposure is dominated by natural sources, including terrestrial gamma radiation and radon-related products. A modest difference between adjacent patches is therefore not automatically evidence of artificial contamination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ukhsa-protectionservices.org.uk/UKionisingradiation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ukhsa-protectionservices.org.uk">[ukhsa-protectionservices.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ukhsa-protectionservices.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ukhsa-protectionservices.org.uk.</span></span></span>

Most importantly, no soil, bark or vegetation samples from the site were taken for laboratory analysis. A laboratory could have identified radionuclides by their energy signatures and tested whether their concentrations were unusual for local soil. The field meter could indicate that ionising radiation was being detected, but it could not tell the team which isotope was responsible, when it arrived or whether it had any connection to the reported lights.

## Did the readings identify a landing site?

The readings offered, at most, a possible clue worth checking. They did not identify a radioactive residue uniquely centred on the depressions, and they did not demonstrate a causal connection between [the meter]({{ 'the-meter/' | relative_url }}) movement and the reported UFO encounter.

For the landing hypothesis to gain strong physical support, the expected evidence would have been a repeatable radiation gradient centred on the alleged contact points; readings clearly exceeding local background and instrument uncertainty; confirmation with a second calibrated detector; and samples showing an unusual isotope or concentration. The Rendlesham survey produced none of these.

Later descriptions sometimes call the readings several times or even ten times normal background. Such ratios can sound dramatic while concealing how small the underlying numbers were. Moving from 0.03 to 0.07 mR/h is a noticeable proportional increase, but it remains a difference of only 0.04 mR/h measured near the bottom of an analogue field meter. A ratio alone cannot establish significance without uncertainty estimates, repeated observations and properly matched controls.

The Ministry of Defence assessed Halt’s memorandum as containing nothing of [defence significance]({{ 'defence-significance/' | relative_url }}) and took no further action. In a 1997 parliamentary answer, the government stated that it had no record of an official technical assessment specifically devoted to the radiation readings. The readings were therefore reported to the Ministry, but they were not validated by a formal government radiation investigation at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hansard.parliament.uk/lords/1997-10-14/debates/96394ce5-f0db-4448-af50-43a69fbf3c57/LieutenantColonelCharlesHaltMemorandum" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hansard.parliament.uk">[Hansard]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hansard.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hansard Lieutenant Colonel Charles Halt: Memorandum</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hansard Lieutenant Colonel Charles Halt: Memorandum</span></span></span>

This also means that two opposite claims should be avoided. It is too strong to say the measurements proved extraordinary contamination; it is also unnecessary to insist that every pointer movement was meaningless. The survey may have detected genuine variation. What it could not determine was whether that variation was unusual, persistent or connected to the incident.

## The limit of the physical evidence

The radiation survey remains interesting because it was conducted during the event by military personnel and partly preserved on a contemporaneous recording. It is one of the few pieces of numerical evidence in the Rendlesham case. That makes it more valuable than a wholly retrospective recollection, but not more precise than the method allowed.

The recorded values were low, irregular and incompletely documented. The instrument was being used at the least informative end of its range, the survey lacked consistent controls, and no independent detector or laboratory samples confirmed an anomalous source. The discrepancy between the exploratory conversation on the tape and the cleaner pattern in Halt’s memorandum adds further uncertainty.

The readings therefore do not establish that an unknown craft irradiated the forest or landed on the three depressions. They show that a survey meter registered small variations while Halt’s party examined the area. Without the measurement context needed to separate environmental variation from instrument noise and genuine contamination, that is as far as the evidence can reliably go.
