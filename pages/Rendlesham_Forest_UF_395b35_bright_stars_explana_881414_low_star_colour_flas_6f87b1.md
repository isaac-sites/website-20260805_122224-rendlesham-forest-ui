## Introduction

One of the most striking features of a bright star low above the horizon is that it can appear to flash rapidly between red, green, blue and white. To an inexperienced observer this can look highly unusual, especially if the star is exceptionally bright and the atmosphere is unstable. In astronomy, however, this is a well-understood optical effect caused by Earth's atmosphere rather than by changes in the star itself. The combination of atmospheric turbulence, refraction and colour dispersion can produce vivid colour changes that are particularly noticeable in brilliant stars such as [Sirius]({{ 'sirius/' | relative_url }}). This mechanism is relevant to discussions of the later aerial lights reported during the Rendlesham Forest incident because Lieutenant Colonel [Charles Halt]({{ 'charles-halt/' | relative_url }}) described low, "star-like" lights displaying exactly this sequence of colours.

## How Turbulent Air Bends Starlight

Stars are so distant that, to the human eye, each one appears as an almost perfect point of light. Unlike a planet, whose tiny disc averages out small distortions, a star's light is extremely sensitive to changes in the atmosphere.

As starlight enters Earth's atmosphere, it passes through countless moving pockets of air with different temperatures and densities. Each pocket has a slightly different refractive index, meaning it bends light by a slightly different amount. Because these pockets are constantly changing, the apparent position and brightness of the star fluctuate many times each second. Astronomers call this effect **stellar [scintillation]({{ 'scintillation/' | relative_url }})**.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomy.com/observing/why-do-some-stars-appear-to-twinkle-while-others-dont/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomy.com">[Astronomy Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Magazine Why do some stars appear to twinkle while others don’t?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy MagazineWhy do some stars appear to twinkle while others don’t?May 7, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2026</span></span></span>

The same turbulent layers do not simply make a star brighten and dim. They also momentarily redirect different wavelengths of light in slightly different directions. Since red, green and blue light are refracted by slightly different amounts, an observer may briefly receive more of one colour than another. The result is the familiar impression of a star flashing through several colours in rapid succession rather than shining with a constant white light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.space.com/why-do-stars-twinkle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: space.com">[Space]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why do stars twinkle? &#124; Space</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Why do stars twinkle? &#124; SpaceJuly 4, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 2022</span></span></span>

Because the atmospheric cells are changing continuously, these colour flashes are irregular rather than rhythmic. They can occur several times each second and may vary considerably from one night to another depending on local atmospheric conditions.

## Why Colour Effects Intensify Near the Horizon

The effect becomes dramatically stronger when a bright star is close to the horizon.

A star directly overhead sends its light through the minimum possible thickness of atmosphere. A star only ten degrees above the horizon must pass through several times more atmosphere before reaching the observer. Every additional layer of air introduces more opportunities for turbulence and atmospheric dispersion to alter the light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/StarChild/questions/question26.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov">[StarChild]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Star Child Why do stars twinkle?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Star Child Why do stars twinkle?</span></span></span>

Several factors combine to make low stars especially dramatic:

* **Longer atmospheric path:** More air means more refraction and stronger scintillation.
* **Greater turbulence:** Near the ground, temperature gradients, wind shear and mixing between warm and cool air create rapidly changing refractive conditions.
* **Enhanced colour separation:** Atmospheric dispersion becomes stronger at low elevations, making individual colours easier to distinguish.
* **High intrinsic brightness:** Very [bright stars]({{ 'bright-stars/' | relative_url }}) produce enough light for the eye to perceive rapid colour changes that would be difficult to notice in fainter stars.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.space.com/why-do-stars-twinkle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: space.com">[space.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">space.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why do stars twinkle? &#124; Space</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Why do stars twinkle? &#124; SpaceJuly 4, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 2022</span></span></span>

Sirius is particularly famous for this behaviour. As the brightest star in the night sky, it is widely recognised by astronomers and experienced observers as capable of producing spectacular flashes of red, green, blue and white when viewed low in the sky under turbulent conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://osr.org/en-uk/blog/astronomy-uk/why-do-stars-twinkle-the-science-behind-the-sparkle/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osr.org">[Online Star Register]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osr.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Star Register Why Do Stars Twinkle? The Science Behind the Sparkle</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Online Star RegisterWhy Do Stars Twinkle? The Science Behind the Sparkle - Online Star Register...</span></span></span>

The phenomenon is sufficiently common that amateur astronomy organisations frequently use Sirius as the standard example when explaining why a stationary star can appear unusually colourful.

## How the Effect Compares with Halt's Account

In his memorandum describing the later stages of the Rendlesham investigation, Halt referred to "star-like" objects roughly ten degrees above the horizon that displayed red, green and blue colours. Those observational details closely match the conditions under which stellar scintillation is strongest.

The correspondence is not simply that both descriptions mention coloured lights. The combination of factors is significant:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the lights were described as **star-like**;
* they were reported **low above the horizon**;
* the colours changed between **red, green and blue**;
* the lights remained visible for an extended period rather than quickly disappearing.

</div>

These characteristics are consistent with the appearance of a bright star observed through unstable winter air. The colour changes themselves do not require the star to be physically changing colour, nor do they imply an unusual atmospheric or astronomical event. They arise naturally from Earth's turbulent atmosphere acting as a constantly shifting optical system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.astronomy.com/observing/why-do-some-stars-appear-to-twinkle-while-others-dont/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astronomy.com">[astronomy.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astronomy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Magazine Why do some stars appear to twinkle while others don’t?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Astronomy MagazineWhy do some stars appear to twinkle while others don’t?May 7, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2026</span></span></span>

At the same time, this explanation addresses only one aspect of Halt's observations. Stellar scintillation explains why a bright, low star can flash rapidly through red, green and blue, but it does not by itself account for every element of the broader Rendlesham narrative, such as later interpretations of apparent movement or reported beams of light. Its strength lies in explaining the specific colour-changing behaviour of low, bright, star-like lights under normal atmospheric conditions rather than serving as a complete explanation for every reported observation.

    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
