## Introduction

Could walking make a stationary light appear to move? Yes—under the right viewing conditions, it can. The visual effect is known as **motion parallax**: as an observer changes position, nearby objects shift across the field of view much more rapidly than distant ones. In a forest at night, tree trunks, branches and gaps between them become a moving foreground screen. A distant fixed light may then seem to slide sideways, jump between openings, or briefly appear to advance or retreat, even if its true bearing changes very little. This mechanism does not prove that every light reported during the Rendlesham Forest incident was stationary, but it provides a well-established perceptual explanation for some reported lateral movements that does not require the light itself to manoeuvre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://astro.unl.edu/naap/distance/parallax.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: astro.unl.edu">[AstroUnl]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">astro.unl.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Astro Unl Parallax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Parallax - Cosmic Distance Ladder - NAAP...</span></span></span>

## What Parallax Looks Like Among Close Tree Trunks

Motion parallax is a normal consequence of perspective rather than an optical illusion in the narrow sense. When a person walks sideways or turns their head, nearby objects appear to move rapidly across the visual scene, while distant objects move much less or appear almost fixed. The brain uses this difference as an important cue for judging depth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocular_vision" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monocular vision</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monocular vision</span></span></span>

Inside a conifer plantation, however, this depth cue can become misleading when the object of interest is visible only through narrow gaps between trunks. As the observer moves:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* one trunk hides the light while another gap reveals it;
* the visible point shifts from one opening to the next;
* the light appears to "slide" sideways despite remaining in essentially the same compass direction;
* repeated disappearances and reappearances can reinforce the impression that the light itself is changing position.

</div>

The effect is strongest because the foreground trees are only a few metres away, whereas the light source may be several kilometres distant. Even a small sideways step changes the alignment between observer, tree trunks and the distant light much more than intuition often suggests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/computer-science/motion-parallax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Motion Parallax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Motion Parallax - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

## How Head Movement Alters a Distant Light's Apparent Path

Walking through woodland rarely involves smooth, straight motion. People naturally weave around trees, step over uneven ground and continually turn their heads. Each movement produces a slightly different line of sight.

At night, several additional factors make these apparent shifts more convincing:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the surrounding landscape is largely invisible except where illuminated;
* few stable reference points remain available;
* individual glimpses are separated by moments when the light disappears behind vegetation;
* the observer's memory fills in the missing portions of the apparent trajectory.

</div>

Instead of perceiving a fixed beacon intermittently obscured by trees, an observer may perceive a single luminous object weaving left and right. Because the interruptions occur while the observer is also moving, the brain can attribute the changing alignment to the light rather than to the foreground obstacles. This is an ordinary consequence of motion parallax combined with intermittent occlusion rather than evidence that the light has physically accelerated or changed course.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocular_vision" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Monocular vision</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monocular vision</span></span></span>

## Which Reported Motions Fit This Effect?

Several descriptions associated with the Rendlesham observations are broadly consistent with what motion parallax would predict.

Reports that a light:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/StarChild/questions/parallax.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov">[starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">starchild.gsfc.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* moved sideways while remaining roughly ahead of the witnesses;
* appeared to shift between trees;
* repeatedly vanished and reappeared;
* seemed difficult to approach despite walking towards it;

</div>

are all compatible with an observer changing position relative to a distant stationary light viewed through foreground vegetation. They do not uniquely identify parallax as the cause, but neither do they require the source itself to have performed corresponding manoeuvres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>

By contrast, claims involving large angular movements across open sky, rapid vertical ascents, or simultaneous observations from substantially different viewpoints are less readily explained by motion parallax alone. Those descriptions require separate evaluation because the mechanism only predicts apparent shifts caused by changes in the observer's own position.

## Why This Matters for the Rendlesham Debate

One long-standing sceptical interpretation proposes that at least some of the reported lights were associated with the Orfordness lighthouse. Contemporary [witness statements]({{ 'witness-dates/' | relative_url }}) include remarks that the light lay in the lighthouse's direction, and several witnesses reported realising that the apparent object was farther away than first believed. Analyses of Lieutenant Colonel [Charles Halt]({{ 'charles-halt/' | relative_url }})'s audio recording have also noted that the recurring flashes occur at intervals close to the lighthouse's flash cycle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendlesham_Forest_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Rendlesham Forest incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Rendlesham Forest incident</span></span></span>

Motion parallax strengthens this interpretation without proving it. If a distant [lighthouse]({{ 'lighthouse/' | relative_url }}) beam was intermittently visible through gaps in the plantation, witnesses walking through the forest would not simply see a flashing light. They would also experience continual changes in its apparent lateral position as foreground trees slid across their view. The combination of intermittent [visibility]({{ 'visibility/' | relative_url }}) and observer movement could therefore produce an impression of a light that was searching, drifting or pacing the observers despite remaining geographically fixed.

Critics correctly note that visibility of the lighthouse depended on the exact observation point, intervening vegetation and local topography. Those factors do not undermine the role of parallax; rather, they are what make the effect plausible. Motion parallax requires an uneven foreground that repeatedly interrupts the line of sight, exactly the kind of visual environment created by a dense conifer plantation at night.

## What Parallax Can—and Cannot—Explain

Motion parallax is best viewed as a mechanism that influences [perception]({{ 'perception/' | relative_url }}) rather than a universal explanation for every reported feature of the Rendlesham incident.

It can plausibly account for:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* apparent sideways movement while observers are walking;
* lights jumping between gaps in the trees;
* repeated disappearances and reappearances;
* difficulty judging distance to isolated lights.

</div>

It cannot, by itself, explain every reported observation, particularly claims of dramatic changes in altitude, structured craft at close range, or independently verified movement across widely separated viewpoints.

For that reason, parallax is most useful as a caution against interpreting perceived lateral motion as direct evidence that a distant light physically changed course. In the forest environment of Rendlesham, observer movement and foreground trees were sufficient to create convincing impressions of motion even when the source itself remained stationary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/computer-science/motion-parallax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Motion Parallax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Motion Parallax - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>
